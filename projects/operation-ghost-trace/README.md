# OPERATION GHOST TRACE

## Case File 001: The Disappearance of Alex Morgan

**Project Type:** Simulated OSINT Investigation
**Classification:** Cybersecurity Portfolio Project
**Status:** Completed — Simulation
**Author:** Dennis Bennett
**Focus Areas:** Open-Source Intelligence, Investigative Research, Evidence Assessment, Digital Footprints, Technical Documentation

---

## 1. Executive Summary

OPERATION GHOST TRACE was a fictional, scenario-based investigation designed to explore how an OSINT investigator might approach a missing-person scenario involving conflicting digital records, a professional dispute, uncertain identity attribution, and potentially significant gaps in the available evidence.

The scenario centred on Alex Morgan, a fictional freelance cybersecurity consultant scheduled to present research at the fictional Northstar Technology Summit. Morgan failed to attend the conference, prompting concerns from a professional contact.

The investigation examined a simulated collection of project correspondence, document histories, access logs, calendar records, account activity, source-handling documentation, and witness statements.

The evidence gradually revealed a dispute concerning the provenance and interpretation of restricted research material. The investigation also identified inconsistencies in meeting arrangements and administrative records that warranted further examination.

A later simulated communication provided a plausible explanation for Morgan's absence. According to the account attributed to Morgan, they had deliberately withdrawn from the project and reduced contact after losing confidence in how their research contribution was being handled.

The scenario concluded with contact reportedly being re-established through a professional associate.

**The principal outcome was a documented investigative hypothesis supported by a structured evidence trail, rather than an assumption that suspicious activity necessarily established criminal conduct.**

All individuals, organisations, records, communications, and investigative findings in this case study are fictional. The evidence was created for the simulation; no real missing-person investigation was conducted.

---

## 2. Investigation Objectives

The investigation was designed to answer five principal questions:

1. What circumstances might explain Alex Morgan's failure to attend the conference?
2. Was there a connection between the disappearance and an ongoing professional dispute?
3. Which digital records could establish the sequence of relevant events?
4. How could conflicting or incomplete evidence be assessed without drawing unsupported conclusions?
5. What additional evidence would be required to distinguish a plausible hypothesis from a verified finding?

A secondary objective was to practise communicating investigative results in a format suitable for a cybersecurity portfolio.

---

## 3. Scenario Background

Alex Morgan was described within the fictional scenario as a freelance cybersecurity consultant and OSINT researcher.

Morgan was scheduled to present a conference session titled *The Invisible Footprint: What Public Data Reveals* at the Northstar Technology Summit on 14 October 2026.

When Morgan failed to attend, a professional contact identified as WIT-014 raised concerns.

Initial records suggested that the expected presenter had completed preparations for the conference. However, the investigation found no reliable confirmation that Morgan had checked in at the event, collected a conference badge, or arrived at the associated hotel.

These discrepancies established the initial investigative problem but did not, independently, establish that Morgan was missing, endangered, or the victim of a crime.

A separate line of investigation concerned a historical attribution project conducted by the fictional organisation Meridian Research Services.

Records associated with that project contained unresolved questions about the provenance of contextual information used to support a relationship between two identifiers.

The possible connection between the research dispute and Morgan's absence became the central investigative hypothesis.

## 4. Methodology

The investigation followed a structured, iterative approach.

### 4.1 Timeline reconstruction

A chronological timeline was developed to identify relationships between significant events, including:

* Conference preparations and communications.
* Historical research activity.
* Changes to the disputed attribution assessment.
* Correspondence concerning source provenance.
* The proposed meeting between Morgan and WIT-014.
* Changes to the meeting location and subsequent cancellation.
* Recorded activity at the building's service entrance.
* Later communications attributed to Morgan.

Timeline reconstruction helped identify contradictions and prioritise subsequent investigative steps.

### 4.2 Digital evidence examination

The simulated evidence collection included:

* Email correspondence and partial message headers.
* Document metadata and revision histories.
* Calendar records and audit events.
* Access-control records.
* Telephone call-detail records.
* Contributor registers and source-handling documentation.
* Account-session events.
* Witness statements and follow-up communications.

Each record was assessed according to what it could reasonably establish.

For example, an account audit could establish that an event was recorded against an account, but not necessarily identify the human operator. Similarly, an access credential could establish a recorded access event without conclusively identifying the person who used it.

### 4.3 Evidence correlation

Separate records were compared to identify corroboration, contradictions, and investigative gaps.

The principal areas of correlation were:

1. The disputed research attribution and its documented limitations.
2. The identity and handling of the original contributor.
3. The sequence of meeting changes and cancellations.
4. Access activity associated with the meeting location.
5. Communications attributed to Morgan before and after the conference.

Particular attention was paid to distinguishing direct evidence from inference.

### 4.4 Hypothesis development

The investigation considered multiple explanations rather than assuming that the most suspicious interpretation was correct.

Potential explanations included:

* A voluntary withdrawal from the project.
* A professional dispute affecting conference participation.
* Errors or inconsistencies in administrative records.
* Unauthorised or unexplained use of an account.
* Deliberate interference with the planned meeting.
* A connection between the project dispute and Morgan's disappearance.

These possibilities were not equally supported, and some remained unresolved at the conclusion of the simulation.

### 4.5 Iterative investigation

Each new finding informed the next investigative step.

Rather than treating the first plausible explanation as the answer, the investigation returned to the evidence when new records complicated the emerging theory.

This process demonstrated the importance of maintaining an open hypothesis until sufficiently corroborated information becomes available.

## 5. Key Findings

### Finding 1: The research attribution had unresolved evidential limitations

The simulated project records showed that contextual information had been supplied to support a possible relationship between two identifiers.

The original source-handling documentation stated that the information had not been independently verified and should not be treated as conclusive proof.

Despite these limitations, a later assessment presented the relationship with greater certainty.

Document revision histories indicated that the wording was strengthened and some references to the source limitations were reduced.

**Assessment:** The records supported a substantive dispute concerning the evidential basis and presentation of the attribution.

They did not establish that the underlying attribution was necessarily false or that the changes constituted deliberate misconduct.

### Finding 2: The contributor's restrictions were documented

The simulated source register linked a historical contact record associated with Alex Morgan to a restricted contributor record.

The documentation indicated that the contributor had requested limitations on how the contextual material could be used and represented.

However, the records did not independently establish the contributor's legal identity.

**Assessment:** The project documentation supported a connection between the historical contact and the restricted contributor record, but the identity verification remained incomplete.

### Finding 3: The planned meeting changed unexpectedly

Calendar audit records showed that the meeting location was changed to a service entrance before the meeting was cancelled.

The changes were recorded against an account associated with the fictional project-coordination function.

Subsequent access records showed activity involving credentials assigned to two project-associated individuals.

A facilities enquiry also concerned whether an expected visitor had entered through the service entrance.

**Assessment:** The sequence justified further investigation, but it did not establish that Morgan attended the location or that either credential holder personally interacted with Morgan.

### Finding 4: Account activity did not establish human identity

The investigation examined account-session events, telephone records, and administrative access logs.

These records contained useful timestamps and account-level associations. However, several lacked complete device identifiers, reliable operator attribution, or independent authentication.

**Assessment:** The investigation could establish that particular events had been recorded, but could not always establish who initiated them.

This distinction was essential to avoiding unsupported accusations.

### Finding 5: A new communication changed the investigation

During the final stage of the simulation, WIT-014 reported receiving an unexpected message referencing the restricted source identifier.

The witness promptly preserved and reported the message.

Subsequent simulated communications offered a potential explanation for Morgan's withdrawal and proposed a method of re-establishing contact.

A later live conversation, as reported by WIT-014, was considered consistent with their previous contact with Morgan.

**Assessment:** Within the scenario, the new communication provided a plausible route to resolving the central question.

However, the sender's real-world identity and precise whereabouts were not independently verified.

## 7. Competing Hypotheses

The investigation's principal hypotheses were assessed qualitatively.

| Hypothesis                                          | Supporting considerations                                                             | Limitations                                                                  |
| --------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Morgan voluntarily withdrew from the project        | The source dispute, documented concerns, and later communication attributed to Morgan | The sender's identity and movements were not independently verified          |
| The project dispute contributed to Morgan's absence | The timing of the dispute, revised attribution, and meeting arrangements              | Timing and association do not independently prove causation                  |
| Someone deliberately interfered with the meeting    | The unexplained location change, cancellation, and access activity                    | No reliable evidence established the responsible operator or intent          |
| Morgan was physically harmed or abducted            | The initial uncertainty and suspicious circumstances                                  | No direct evidence of an attack, abduction, or physical harm was established |

The strongest supported explanation within the simulation was that Morgan had withdrawn from the project and reduced contact amid concerns about the handling of the research material.

The evidence did not justify concluding that a crime had occurred.

## 8. Final Assessment

The scenario began with an unexplained absence and developed into an investigation of conflicting digital records and a professional research dispute.

The most significant finding was not an individual access event or suspicious timestamp. It was the relationship between the original source restrictions, the revised attribution, and the subsequent communications concerning the contributor's withdrawal.

The simulated records supported the conclusion that the attribution was presented more confidently than its documented evidential basis warranted.

They also supported a plausible explanation for the contributor's decision to withdraw from the project.

However, several questions remained unresolved:

* The independent identity of the person communicating as Alex Morgan.
* The identity of the person who made the facilities enquiry.
* The reason for the meeting-location change.
* Whether any project participant deliberately attempted to pressure the contributor.
* Morgan's independently verified location.

The case therefore concluded with a substantially developed investigative hypothesis rather than a claim that every aspect of the disappearance had been conclusively established.

**Final status:** Substantially resolved within the simulation; residual questions remain.

## 9. Skills Demonstrated

This project provided an opportunity to practise and document the following skills:

* **OSINT methodology:** Structuring an investigation around specific questions and evidence.
* **Digital footprint analysis:** Examining the significance and limitations of account activity.
* **Timeline reconstruction:** Correlating events across multiple simulated records.
* **Metadata interpretation:** Assessing document histories, timestamps, and account associations.
* **Evidence correlation:** Comparing independent records to identify corroboration and discrepancies.
* **Hypothesis testing:** Considering alternative explanations and revising conclusions when new information appeared.
* **Critical thinking:** Distinguishing suspicious circumstances from verified facts.
* **Investigative documentation:** Producing a structured report with findings, limitations, and a final assessment.
* **Ethical OSINT practice:** Recognising the risks of misidentification and unsupported allegations.

This was a scenario-based exercise rather than a live technical investigation. It did not involve exploiting systems, bypassing access controls, or accessing private accounts.

## 10. Lessons Learned

### 10.1 Account attribution is not human attribution

A log entry associated with a named account does not automatically establish who performed the action.

Investigators should consider authentication strength, device information, session continuity, and the possibility of shared access.

### 10.2 Correlation does not establish causation

The fact that a professional dispute preceded an absence does not prove that the dispute caused the absence.

A defensible report should distinguish chronological association from an established causal relationship.

### 10.3 Preserve uncertainty explicitly

Missing records, incomplete headers, ambiguous identities, and unverified statements should remain visible in the final assessment.

Removing uncertainty from a report can make a hypothesis appear more conclusive than the evidence warrants.

### 10.4 New evidence should change the investigation

The later communication from WIT-014 introduced a new investigative direction.

This demonstrated why investigators should remain open to revising a hypothesis instead of continuing to pursue a single theory regardless of new information.

### 10.5 A good investigation includes stopping points

Not every question can be resolved from the available evidence.

A professional conclusion should explain what has been established, what remains uncertain, and what additional evidence would be required to proceed.

---

## 11. Ethical Considerations and Limitations

This case study is entirely fictional.

All people, organisations, records, account identifiers, communications, access events, and findings were invented for the training scenario. They should not be interpreted as allegations concerning real individuals or organisations.

No real missing-person investigation was conducted. No actual CCTV, building-access system, telephone database, email account, private correspondence, or organisational records were accessed.

The scenario's final communications and reported re-establishment of contact were also simulated.

Consequently, the findings demonstrate an investigative reasoning process rather than verified facts about a real event.

In a real investigation, identity claims and critical findings would require appropriate independent corroboration. Access to non-public records would require lawful authority and suitable permissions.

The report deliberately avoids identifying a perpetrator or asserting that a crime occurred where the evidence does not establish either conclusion.

---

## 12. Conclusion

OPERATION GHOST TRACE demonstrated how an investigator can organise a complex scenario involving digital records, disputed source provenance, conflicting timelines, and uncertain identity attribution.

The exercise highlighted the importance of evidence correlation, cautious interpretation, iterative hypothesis development, and transparent reporting.

Its central lesson is that effective OSINT investigation is not simply about finding suspicious information. It is about determining what that information actually establishes, identifying what remains unknown, and communicating the difference clearly.
